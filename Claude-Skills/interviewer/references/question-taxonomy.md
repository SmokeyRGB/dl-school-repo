# Question Taxonomy

Two source frameworks, combined. Use both when planning and asking questions.

## Open-ended vs. closed-ended (IREB CPRE)

- **Closed-ended**: answer is given/defined by the question ("Do you currently
  use a chore app?"). Produces quantitative, easy-to-process data. Use only
  when you specifically need to confirm or quantify something (e.g. checking
  whether one person's claim generalizes to others).
- **Open-ended**: allows a free response, surfaces narrative/argumentative
  knowledge ("What happens when a flatmate skips their chores?"). Produces
  qualitative data. This is the default mode for elicitation — most questions
  in a discovery interview should be open-ended.

## Kvale's nine question types (*Doing Interviews*, Box 5.2)

Roughly ordered from least to most directive. Prefer the top of the list
early in a topic; hold the bottom for later, once the interviewee has given
their own spontaneous framing.

| # | Type | What it does | Example |
|---|------|---------------|---------|
| A | Introductory | Open, low-pressure opener that invites spontaneous description | "Can you tell me about the last time chores caused friction in your flat?" |
| B | Follow-up | Extends an answer — often just repeating the interviewee's own significant word back | "You said it felt like 'chaos' — what does that look like day to day?" |
| C | Probing | Pushes for more without specifying which angle | "Could you say more about that?" / "Do you have another example?" |
| D | Specifying | Narrows toward concrete, operational detail | "What did you actually do the last time that happened?" |
| E | Direct | States the topic/dimension outright | "Have you ever tried a chore-splitting app?" |
| F | Indirect | Projective phrasing — can surface an attitude the person won't state directly | "How do you think your flatmates would describe this problem?" |
| G | Structuring | Manages pacing — closes an exhausted/irrelevant thread and names the next topic | "Got it, that's clear. Let's shift to how you'd know it's fixed." |
| H | Silence | A deliberate pause instead of another question, giving room to volunteer more | *(say nothing for a beat)* |
| I | Interpreting | Reflects a paraphrase back to check understanding | "So if I understand right, the real issue isn't the chores — it's that nobody agreed on them upfront?" |

**Linguistic axes (Box 5.3)** — the same request for elaboration can be phrased
along different registers, and staying in one register the whole interview
shapes the whole conversation's character:

- Descriptive/behavioural/experiential: "What did you do?" / "How did you
  experience it?"
- Emotional/cognitive/evaluative: "How did that feel?" / "What's your take
  on it now?"

Mix registers deliberately rather than defaulting to one.

## Ordering: what/how before why

Resolve *why* and *what* for yourself during preparation, but *inside* the
interview lead with descriptive "what happened" / "how did you experience
it" questions. Save "why" questions for late, if at all — they invite
over-intellectualized or defensive answers, or feel like an oral exam.
Explaining *why* someone feels or acts a certain way is analysis work done
after the interview, not something to extract directly by asking it.
(Kvale's analogy: a doctor asks what's wrong and what the symptoms are, not
why the patient is sick, and draws the diagnosis from the answers.)

## Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Leading questions** — the phrasing implies the "right" answer
  ("Wouldn't it be great if the app just split chores automatically?").
- **Compound/stacked questions** — asking two things at once forces the
  interviewee to pick one, usually the easier one.
- **Closed questions used as if they were open** — "Is that a problem for
  you?" invites a one-word answer; "What happens when that comes up?" does not.
- **Premature direct/why questions** — naming your hypothesis before the
  interviewee has offered their own framing anchors their answer to your words.
- **Filling every pause** — silence (type H) is a legitimate move, not dead air.
- **Not following up on loaded words** — an interviewee's own charged term
  (Kvale's example: "red star") is usually the richest thread in the
  interview; repeating it back is often better than asking a new question.
