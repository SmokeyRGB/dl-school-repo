# Notes: "Conducting an Interview"

Source: Steinar Kvale, *Doing Interviews* (SAGE, 2007), Chapter 5, pp. 50–65.
These are condensed study notes in my own words, not a transcript — for the original wording, see the source book.

## Chapter objectives

The chapter aims to help the reader understand:

- how knowledge gets produced *through* an interview, not just extracted from it;
- interaction and questioning technique, illustrated via a demonstration interview;
- how to prepare an interview — setting the stage, and scripting it with an interview guide;
- the different roles of researcher questions vs. interviewer questions, question wording, and the skill of following up ("second questions").

Kvale frames interviewing as a **craft learned through practice**, not a method with fixed rules — the chapter that precedes this one lists the seven stages of an interview inquiry (thematizing, designing, interviewing, transcribing, analyzing, verifying, reporting), and argues that investment in the early stages (thematizing, design) pays off in higher-quality, easier-to-analyze interviews later.

## The demonstration interview (Box 5.1)

Kvale includes a real, lightly-edited transcript of a ten-minute interview he conducted live in front of a class at Saybrook Institute (San Francisco, 1987), with an adult volunteer recalling her experience of grades in primary school. It's used as a worked example, not as data in itself.

Key moves he makes as interviewer:

- Opens with a concrete, low-pressure question ("do you remember the first time...") rather than an abstract one.
- Follows up on emotionally loaded words the interviewee uses herself — "red star," "mixed emotions," "rewarded" — by simply repeating them back, which invites elaboration without leading the answer.
- Draws out a specific tension the interviewee names (being a "teacher's pet" vs. loyalty to peers) and probes whether it's a recurring pattern in her life.
- Closes with a debriefing: asks if there's anything else she'd like to add, then afterward asks how she experienced *being interviewed* about the topic.

Afterward, Kvale annotates his own questions against three theoretical lenses he'd set out to explore (from earlier in the book): a **Rogerian** move (reflecting feelings back), a **Freudian** move (probing for family/childhood roots of the conflict), and a **Skinnerian** move (probing what "rewarded" concretely meant — TV, ice cream). The point: the same short interview can be read through multiple theoretical frames, and awareness of those frames shapes which follow-ups an interviewer chooses to chase.

## Setting the interview stage

- The first minutes matter most — interviewees need to feel a rapport with the interviewer before they'll open up about personal experience.
- Every interview should have a **briefing** (explain purpose, recording, consent, invite questions) and a **debriefing** (ask if there's anything else to add; optionally share back what the interviewer took from it). The debriefing matters because interviewees can feel exposed or oddly empty after disclosing personal material — but this openness afterward, once the recorder is off, is also common and can surface things they didn't feel safe saying on tape.
- Kvale recommends the interviewer take ~10 minutes of quiet reflection immediately after each interview to capture impressions that the transcript alone won't preserve (tone, body language, mood) — useful context for later analysis.

## Scripting the interview

- An **interview guide** ranges from a loose list of topics to a tightly sequenced set of questions, depending on the study.
- Useful practice: keep **two separate lists** —
  1. *Researcher questions*: the theoretical/academic version of what you want to know.
  2. *Interviewer questions*: the same thing translated into plain, colloquial language the interviewee will actually respond to.
  
  A worked example (Table 5.1, from a study on grades and motivation) shows one researcher question ("does learning for grades socialize students to working for wages?") branching into several plain-language interview questions ("have you been rewarded with money for good grades?", "do you see any connection between money and grades?").
- On **why/what/how**: when designing the study, resolve *why* and *what* first, then figure out *how* to ask. But *inside* the interview, flip that — lead with descriptive "what happened," "how did you experience it" questions; save "why" questions (which invite over-intellectualized, defensive answers, or feel like an oral exam) for later, if at all. Explaining *why* subjects feel/act as they do is treated as primarily the researcher's analytical job, not something to extract directly from the subject.
- Analogy offered: a doctor doesn't open by asking a patient *why* they're sick — they ask what's wrong and what the symptoms are, and draws the diagnosis from that. Interviewers are advised toward the same descriptive-first stance.
- Also: interviews vary in how upfront they are about their purpose — direct from the start, vs. a **funnel-shaped** approach that narrows from general to specific and reveals the real focus only at the end. Kvale flags that funnel-shaped (indirect) interviewing raises informed-consent questions worth thinking through.

## Interviewer questions (Box 5.2) — a taxonomy

Nine question types Kvale distinguishes, roughly in order of how directive they are:

| Type | Purpose |
|---|---|
| A. Introductory | Open, low-pressure prompts ("can you tell me about...") that invite spontaneous, subject-led description |
| B. Follow-up | Extends an answer via curiosity/persistence — direct questioning, a nod, "mm," a pause, or repeating back the interviewee's own words |
| C. Probing | Pursues more detail ("could you say more about that?") without specifying which angle to take |
| D. Specifying | Narrows toward concrete detail ("what did you actually do?") |
| E. Direct | States the topic/dimension outright — best saved for later in the interview, after the subject has already given their own spontaneous framing |
| F. Indirect | Projective phrasing ("how do you think other pupils see it?") — answers may reveal the subject's own attitude without stating it directly, so need careful interpretation |
| G. Structuring | Interviewer manages pacing — politely closes an exhausted or irrelevant thread and signals a topic change |
| H. Silence | Deliberately allowing pauses (borrowed from therapeutic technique) so the subject has room to associate and volunteer more |
| I. Interpreting | Reflecting a paraphrase or interpretation back to check understanding ("do you mean that...?") |

A companion box (5.3) shows how the same request for elaboration can be phrased along different axes — descriptive/behavioral/experiential ("what did you do," "how did you experience it") vs. emotional/cognitive/evaluative ("how did you feel," "what's your opinion now") — and notes that sticking to one register throughout tends to shape the whole interview's character (e.g., a mostly emotional interview vs. a mostly conceptual one).

Kvale's retrospective read of his own demonstration interview: mostly probing and follow-up questions built by repeating the subject's own words back, a few specifying/interpreting questions, and direct questions held back until later — plus at least one thread (the subject's remark about valuing friendship) he consciously chose *not* to chase further, which he reflects on afterward as a judgment call about sensitivity in a classroom setting.

## The art of second questions

- Active listening is treated as being just as important as knowing formal question types — the interviewer must track both *what* is said and *how*.
- Deciding which of many possible threads in an answer to pursue takes topic knowledge, social sensitivity, and a feel for the interview's purpose — compared in the text to chess, where each move reshapes the board and forces the next move to account for it.
- Box 5.4 illustrates this with one interviewee statement about grades being "often unjust," followed by a whole menu of *possible* follow-ups an interviewer could choose from (silence, a minimal "mm," repeating a phrase back, asking for examples, asking for confirmation of an implied feeling, asking a leading/challenging counter-question, etc.) — the point being there's no single "correct" second question, just different answers pulling on different threads of what was said.

## Key points (chapter summary, as given)

- A qualitative interview is usually semi-structured: a planned sequence of themes and some prepared questions, but open to reordering and rephrasing to follow what the interviewee actually says.
- The social interaction of the interview itself shapes both the interviewee's willingness to answer and the quality of what they say.
- Careful attention to staging matters: briefing before, debriefing after, and thinking through what effects the briefing itself has on the knowledge produced and on the interviewee.
- When scripting, it can help to keep two parallel lists: research questions in academic language, and their translation into plain interview questions.
- Interview quality isn't just about the questions asked — how the interviewer *responds* after an answer (pausing, probing, checking understanding) matters just as much.
